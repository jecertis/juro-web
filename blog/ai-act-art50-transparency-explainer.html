EU AI Act Article 50: Transparency Requirements for Compliance Teams

By JuroCompliant · 13 Jun 2026 · Updated 25 Nov 2026 · ~7 min read
Share this article

EU AI Act Article 50 sets binding transparency obligations for providers and deployers of certain AI systems. It has applied since 2 August 2026. Providers of generative AI systems placed on the market before that date were given until 2 December 2026 to meet the machine-readable marking duty in Article 50(2).

If your product uses AI-generated output, synthetic media, or an AI chatbot, work out which of these duties fall on you and keep a dated record of that assessment.

What Does Article 50 Actually Require?

Art. 50 of the EU AI Act sets transparency obligations in its first four paragraphs. Each one names who carries it. Each also has exceptions, including for certain uses authorised by law in relation to criminal offences.

1. Interaction disclosure: providers (Art. 50(1)). Providers must design and develop AI systems intended to interact directly with natural persons, such as chatbots and customer service agents, so that those persons are informed they are interacting with an AI system. The exception is where this is obvious to a reasonably well-informed, observant and circumspect person in the context of use. Under Art. 50(5), the information must be clear and distinguishable and provided at the latest at the time of the first interaction.

2. Synthetic content marking: providers (Art. 50(2)). Providers of AI systems, including general-purpose AI systems, that generate synthetic audio, image, video, or text content must ensure the outputs are marked in a machine-readable format and detectable as artificially generated or manipulated. Their technical solutions must be effective, interoperable, robust and reliable as far as technically feasible. The duty does not apply to the extent a system performs an assistive function for standard editing, or does not substantially alter the input data provided by the deployer or its meaning.

3. Emotion recognition and biometric categorisation: deployers (Art. 50(3)). Deployers of an emotion recognition system or a biometric categorisation system must inform the natural persons exposed to it that the system is operating, and must process personal data in line with the applicable EU data protection law.

4. Deep fakes and public-interest text: deployers (Art. 50(4)). Deployers of an AI system that generates or manipulates image, audio, or video content constituting a deep fake must disclose that the content has been artificially generated or manipulated. Deployers must also disclose this for AI-generated or manipulated text published to inform the public on matters of public interest, unless the content has undergone human review or editorial control and a natural or legal person holds editorial responsibility for its publication.

In short, paragraphs 1 and 2 are provider obligations, and paragraphs 3 and 4 are deployer obligations. Under Art. 3(3), a company that develops an AI system, or has one developed, and places it on the market or puts it into service under its own name may be its provider, including where the system is built on another company's model. Under Art. 3(4), a company using an AI system under its authority in a professional capacity is its deployer. One company can be both, so check each feature separately.

What Is the GPAI Carve-Out?

General-purpose AI models, such as large language models and multimodal foundation models, have a separate set of obligations under Art. 53 and, for models with systemic risk, Art. 55. These started applying on 2 August 2025. Art. 50 addresses AI systems: the products and services that put such capabilities in front of people.

The exclusion to check first is research and development (Art. 2(6) and 2(8)). The Act does not apply to AI systems or models specifically developed and put into service for the sole purpose of scientific research and development. Nor does it apply to research, testing or development activity before a system or model is placed on the market or put into service, although testing in real world conditions is not covered by that second exclusion. A feature you have already shipped is past that pre-market stage.

For evidently artistic, creative, satirical, fictional or analogous works, deep fake disclosure is limited, not removed (Art. 50(4)). The existence of the generated or manipulated content must still be disclosed, in an appropriate manner that does not hamper the display or enjoyment of the work. This limitation sits in the deep fake rule and does not change the provider duties in paragraphs 1 and 2.

When Did Article 50 Start to Apply?

Article 50 has applied since 2 August 2026, the general date of application in Art. 113. The AI Act applies in phases:

  • 1 August 2024: Act entered into force
  • 2 February 2025: Prohibited practices (Art. 5) started applying
  • 2 August 2025: General-purpose AI model obligations (Chapter V) started applying
  • 2 August 2026: Art. 50 transparency obligations started applying
  • 2 December 2026: End of the Art. 50(2) transition for generative AI systems placed on the market before 2 August 2026

The Digital Omnibus on AI, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, was published on 24 July 2026 and entered into force on 27 July 2026. It amended the AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689), adding the 2 December 2026 transition for Art. 50(2) and moving dates for certain high-risk obligations. It did not move the 2 August 2026 date for Art. 50. The European Commission also published a voluntary Code of Practice on marking and labelling AI-generated content on 10 June 2026 and guidelines on the Article 50 obligations on 20 July 2026.

National market surveillance authorities are the main enforcers, and the AI Office is competent for certain AI systems listed in Art. 75(1). Non-compliance with Art. 50 by providers or deployers is subject to administrative fines of up to €15 million or, for undertakings, up to 3% of total worldwide annual turnover for the preceding financial year, whichever is higher (Art. 99(4)(g)). For SMEs, including start-ups, and small mid-cap enterprises, the limit is whichever of the two is lower (Art. 99(6) and (6a)).

What Should Compliance Teams Document Now?

Art. 50 already applies, so the practical work is to document where you stand today and what is still open. A suggested priority order:

Map your AI touchpoints. List every AI-generated or AI-assisted user-facing output your product produces: chatbot responses, recommendation outputs, generated text, synthetic images. If you don't have this list, you can't assess Art. 50 scope.

Check your interaction disclosures. For each AI system that interacts with natural persons, confirm there is an unambiguous disclosure before or at the start of interaction — not a generic "we use AI" buried in an FAQ. The disclosure must be clear to the person in the moment.

Audit your synthetic content labels. If you generate synthetic media — images, audio, video — confirm you have a machine-readable marking mechanism. A visible watermark alone may not satisfy the machine-readable requirement; check what your generation tool provides.

Document your GPAI provider chain. If your product calls a foundation model API, document which model, which version, and which Art. 50 obligations fall on you as provider, deployer, or both. Do not assume your foundation model provider's terms cover them.

Date and sign your assessment. A dated, signed assessment records what you reviewed and when. An undated internal note is weaker evidence if someone later asks when you assessed your Art. 50 position.

How Does This Interact With GDPR?

Art. 50 disclosure obligations are distinct from GDPR transparency requirements under Art. 13/14, but they stack. If your AI system processes personal data and interacts with data subjects, you need GDPR-compliant privacy notices and Art. 50 AI disclosure — both.

For companies also subject to DPDP (India) or DORA (EU financial sector), the documentation burden compounds further. Frequently Asked Questions

Does Article 50 apply to internal AI tools used by employees, not customers?

Art. 50 applies to AI systems deployed with natural persons. For purely internal tools with no user-facing interaction output, the obligation is narrower — but if the tool's outputs reach customers (e.g., AI-drafted customer emails, AI-generated reports shared externally), the disclosure question re-enters. Assess by output destination, not just deployment context.

Does our foundation model provider handle Art. 50 compliance for us?

Do not assume so. Providers of general-purpose AI models have their own obligations under Art. 53, and under Art. 55 for models with systemic risk. Art. 50 duties attach to AI systems: paragraphs 1 and 2 fall on the system's provider, and paragraphs 3 and 4 on its deployer. If your SaaS product uses an LLM API to power a user-facing feature offered under your own name, you may be the provider of that AI system under Art. 3(3), as well as its deployer where you use it under your own authority.

What counts as a machine-readable label for synthetic content?

Art. 50(2) requires providers to ensure outputs are marked in a machine-readable format and detectable as artificially generated or manipulated, using technical solutions that are effective, interoperable, robust and reliable as far as technically feasible. The European Commission published a voluntary Code of Practice on marking and labelling AI-generated content on 10 June 2026. Techniques in use include C2PA (Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity) metadata embedded in media files, visible watermarks plus EXIF/XMP metadata, and explicit API-level flags in output payloads. A disclaimer sentence in a footnote does not satisfy the machine-readable requirement.

We have an AI chatbot — what exactly does our disclosure need to say?

The Act does not mandate specific wording. The disclosure must make clear that the user is interacting with an AI system, before or at the start of the interaction, in a format they can understand. "You are chatting with an AI assistant" placed at the start of the chat interface is the baseline standard. Generic "powered by AI" branding in a product header is unlikely to satisfy the obligation.
